I'm a structural engineer in Pune. Six years of practice, lateral systems, and ETABS models — and a growing conviction that structural engineering logic can be expressed as real, working software.

The experiment

Most structural analysis software is a black box. You enter numbers, results come out, and the math in between is invisible. I wanted to know what would happen if I wrote the math myself — not as a spreadsheet, but as code.

StruxLab started as that experiment. Could I implement strain compatibility from scratch and draw a P-M interaction curve in a browser? Could I assemble a stiffness matrix and solve a multi-span beam without any libraries? Could I derive lateral stiffness for a braced frame from first principles and make it interactive?

The answer, it turns out, is yes. And once you've written it, you understand it in a way that using software never gives you.

Engineering logic is just math with physical meaning. If the math can be written, the software can be built.

What StruxLab is for

The tools and writing here are aimed at engineers who want to understand the engineering, not just run it. Students who've worked through textbook derivations but never watched them move. Junior engineers trying to build intuition before they trust the black box.

Every tool on this site is built around a single concept — and every concept is explained. The diagrams are drawn by the math. The source is readable. If something looks wrong, you can trace it. That's intentional.

I build engineering software on the side: browser-based tools, automation scripts, and technical writing aimed at working engineers. If you're interested in collaborating on that kind of work, I'd like to hear from you.

Background
